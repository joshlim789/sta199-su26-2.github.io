Recap: where we left off

Last lecture: RCTs

  • We showed that randomization is incredibly powerful: it makes \(Z \perp X\), so any difference in \(Y\) between groups must be due to treatment

  • This gave us the license to say causes: “receiving VWG OIT causes an increase in max tolerated wheat protein dose”

  • Today: what happens when we can’t randomize?

When randomization isn’t possible

  • Ethical: you cannot randomly assign people to smoke, to not receive healthcare, or to experience childhood poverty

  • Practical: policy changes happen at scale. You can’t “randomize” a country’s minimum wage law

  • Historical: many causal questions are about events that already happened (retrospective)

  • Most real-world causal questions are answered with observational data.

Observational Studies

What is an Observational Study?

  • In an observational study, treatment is not assigned by the researcher, Subjects choose, or are assigned by nature or circumstance

  • Surveys (including the census)

  • Electronic Health Records

  • Most sources of data can be thought of an observational study! Just have to define what the treatment is

  • One key advantage over randomized experiments is the ease in collecting data! Usually has a much bigger sample size.

The issues

  • \(Z\) is not independent of \(X\): people who receive treatment differ systematically from those who don’t

  • Changes in \(Y\) not directly attributable to changes in \(Z\)

A concrete example

  • Students who seek tutoring tend to be those who are struggling — lower prior GPA

  • If we compare exam scores, tutored students may still score lower on average, even if tutoring genuinely helps

  • The confounder (prior ability) affects both who gets tutoring (\(Z\)) and the outcome (\(Y\))

  • Solution: we need a way to make treated and control groups comparable on \(X\), without running an experiment

Key takeaway

  • In an observational study, a naive difference in group means is not a valid causal estimate

  • We need to adjust for confounders \(X\) to recover the treatment effect

  • The rest of today is about two strategies for doing that

Matching

The idea of matching

recall covariate balance in a randomized study:

randomization balanced the covariates so that the only possible thing that changed was the treatment

  • For each treated unit, find one (or more) control units that look similar on \(X\)

  • Compare outcomes within matched pairs — differences now reflect treatment, not confounding

  • We are trying to mimic a randomized experiment by constructing comparable groups from observational data

  • After matching: treated and control units have similar \(X\) distributions, so \(Z \perp X\) holds approximately

The key assumption

  • Matching only works if we have measured all important confounders

  • Formally: conditional ignorability

    \[Y(0), Y(1) \perp Z \mid X\]

  • Given \(X\), treatment assignment is “as good as random”

  • Also called no unmeasured confounders — a strong, untestable assumption

  • This is the observational equivalent of randomization: we can’t guarantee it, we have to argue for it

The LaLonde Study

Context

LaLonde (1986) evaluated the National Supported Work (NSW) program: a 1970s job training intervention for disadvantaged workers.

  • Cohort: Disadvantaged workers (long-term unemployed, ex-offenders, welfare recipients)

  • Design: The original NSW had a randomized arm, but LaLonde compared its estimates against observational comparison groups drawn from national surveys — showing how badly naive methods can fail

  • Treatment (\(Z = 1\)): Participation in the NSW job training program

  • Control (\(Z = 0\)): Non-participants drawn from observational comparison groups

  • Outcome (\(Y\)): Earnings in 1978 (re78)

  • Covariates (\(X\)): Age, education, race, marital status, prior earnings (re74, re75)

What the imbalance tells us

  • Treated individuals have substantially lower prior earnings (re74, re75) than controls

  • The control group (from national surveys) is richer and more educated on average

  • A naive comparison overstates the earnings gap — the treated group is catching up from a lower baseline, not just benefiting from training

  • We need to adjust for these differences before drawing causal conclusions

Propensity scores

The curse of dimensionality

  • Covariate matching works well when \(X\) has only a few variables

  • With many covariates, it becomes nearly impossible to find good matches — the covariate space is too sparse

  • Example: match exactly on age, education, prior earnings, race, marital status, region, …

  • The probability of finding a near-identical control unit vanishes as the number of covariates grows

  • We need a smarter approach

The propensity score

  • Instead of matching on the full \(X\) vector, collapse it to a single number

  • The propensity score:

    \[e(X) = P(Z = 1 \mid X)\]

  • The probability of receiving treatment, given covariates

  • Rosenbaum & Rubin (1983): if \(Y(0), Y(1) \perp Z \mid X\), then also \(Y(0), Y(1) \perp Z \mid e(X)\)

  • Matching on this one number is sufficient!

Key takeaway

  • The propensity score reduces a high-dimensional matching problem to a one-dimensional one

  • We estimate \(e(X)\) with logistic regression, then match on the predicted probability

  • Balance on \(e(X)\) implies (approximate) balance on all of \(X\)

Estimating the propensity score

We estimate \(e(X)\) via logistic regression (which we already know!):

\[\log\frac{P(Z=1 \mid X)}{1 - P(Z=1 \mid X)} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_1 + \cdots + \beta_p X_p\]

  • Fit logistic regression of \(Z\) on \(X\)

  • Predicted probabilities \(\hat{e}(X_i)\) are the estimated propensity scores

  • Match treated and control units with similar \(\hat{e}(X)\)

The workflow

  1. Fit a logistic regression model: \(Z \sim X_1 + \cdots + X_p\)
  2. Extract predicted probabilities as propensity scores
  3. Match treated to control units with similar scores
  4. Check balance on \(X\) in the matched sample
  5. Estimate the treatment effect on the matched data

Step 1 & 2: fit model, get scores

ps_model <- glm(

linear_reg() |>
  fit(re78 ~ treat, data = ps_data) |>
  tidy()
# A tibble: 2 × 5
  term        estimate std.error statistic  p.value
  <chr>          <dbl>     <dbl>     <dbl>    <dbl>
1 (Intercept)    5988.      533.    11.2   2.38e-25
2 treat           362.      754.     0.479 6.32e- 1

  • After propensity score matching, we have a valid causal estimate (under ignorability)

  • This scales to many covariates, unlike direct covariate matching

  • Always check balance after matching — if balance is poor, improve the propensity score model

Limitations of observational studies

  • No unmeasured confounders: the key assumption is untestable — if an important confounder is missing from \(X\), our estimate is still biased

  • Bad controls: we need to choose \(X\) carefully — only include pre-treatment variables, never variables caused by \(Z\)

  • Generalizability: matching typically estimates the treatment effect on the treated (ATT), not the full population

  • Model dependence: the propensity score model matters — wrong specification leads to poor balance and biased estimates

  • RCTs are still the gold standard; observational methods are our best option when experiments are impossible

Key takeaways

  • Observational studies require careful adjustment for confounders \(X\)

  • Covariate matching: find control units similar on \(X\) directly — works well with few covariates

  • Propensity score matching: match on \(P(Z = 1 \mid X)\) — scales to high dimensions

  • Both require no unmeasured confounders — an assumption that must be argued for, not tested from data

  • When you see an observational study making a causal claim, ask: what confounders might they have missed?
